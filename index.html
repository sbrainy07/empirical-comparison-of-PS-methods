Empirical Comparison of Tree-Based Machine Learning Models for Estimating Generalized Propensity Scores in Multi-Category Treatment Settings

Oluwaseun Farotimi, Ph.D.•Deborah K. Reed, Ph.D.

View / Download PDF References

Abstract

Propensity score (PS) methods address confounding in observational research by balancing measured covariates across treatment groups. Using an applied teacher professional development (PD) example with three PD types, we compare four tree-based machine learning methods — classification tree (CART), bagging, random forest (RF), and gradient boosted models (GBM) — for estimating generalized propensity scores.

Purpose of the Study

To evaluate the performance of four tree-based ML models (GBM, RF, CART, and Bagging) in PS estimation with three treatment conditions for large-scale statewide teacher professional development.

Literature Review

  • In quasi-experimental designs, assignment to condition is non-randomized (e.g., self-selection), so group differences are more systematic than in RCTs (Shadish et al., 2002).
  • Observation studies are subject to treatment selection bias, which is a principal threat to valid causal inference (Nguyen et al., 2021).
  • PS methods let researchers control how comparison groups are formed while minimizing selection bias and confounding.
  • Existing PS literature focuses on binary treatment–control comparisons; few studies examine multiple treatments (Leite et al., 2025; Westreich et al., 2020).
  • ML methods can improve covariate balance and reduce bias relative to traditional parametric approaches (Lee et al., 2010; Westreich et al., 2010).

Research Question & Data

Main RQ: How well do different tree-based ML models achieve covariate balance across multiple treatments?

Data: 19,065 kindergarteners and 1,254 teachers in two large urban districts who completed the Fall 2023 and Spring 2024 universal reading screener (URS) administrations.

Theoretical Framework

The PS is a common approach for estimating causal treatment effects in observation data (Lopez & Gutman, 2017); ML offers more robust estimation by capturing nonlinear relationships among covariates (Leite et al., 2025).

  • GBM iteratively adds decision-tree learners via gradient boosting to improve prediction of treatment assignment (Setodji et al., 2017).
  • RF averages class probabilities across many trees grown on bootstrap samples and random predictor subsets (Breiman, 2001).
  • CART recursively splits data into homogeneous subsets using the best separating rule at each node (Penning de Vries et al., 2018).
  • Bagging fits a tree to each bootstrap sample and averages predictions to reduce variance (Liaw & Wiener, 2002).

Findings

  • GBM and RF achieved the best overall covariate balance after weighting.
  • CART and Bagging produced maximum ASMD above 0.20 and poor balance after weighting.
  • GBM’s sequential optimization of treatment-assignment prediction outperforms the simpler, interpretable CART and Bagging models.

Limitations & Future Work

  • Findings rest on a single observational dataset with a specific covariate structure, treatment type, and sample size.
  • The relative ranking of the four estimators may not extend to other multi-treatment settings.
  • A Monte Carlo simulation is needed to assess stability and identify conditions under which each model performs optimally.
